\begin{abstract}
Automated extraction of chemical reactions from figures is a key
bottleneck on the route from the published literature to the structured
databases on which data-driven chemistry depends. Agentic
vision--language pipelines report state-of-the-art extraction results,
but they are typically evaluated on benchmarks their own prompts and
tools were developed against, and it remains unclear whether extraction
quality is governed by the multimodal model or by the orchestration
around it. Herein, we present a pre-registered, held-out evaluation of
a frozen single-agent pipeline (one model, two chemistry tools, one
prompt) on the public ChemEAGLE benchmark release: 324 figure images,
of which 321 carry released ground truth (2{,}899 reactions), with the
sixteen figures used during pipeline development excluded to yield a
305-image held-out test set on which no prompt, tool, or configuration
was ever iterated. On the held-out set, Claude
Opus~5 reaches a partial-match F1 of 0.740 (95\% CI 0.697--0.783),
Gemini~3.7~Flash 0.629 (0.581--0.676), Gemini~3~Flash 0.615
(0.569--0.661), and GPT-5.4 0.228 (0.190--0.268): a 0.51 spread behind
a byte-identical pipeline. The seven-agent ChemEAGLE reference system
itself, re-run on its complete benchmark under the same scorer, reaches
0.352: half the score of a single agent with a low-cost backbone at the
same cost per image. Hence, the multimodal model, not the
orchestration, decides extraction quality at benchmark scale. Furthermore, it is
found that serving reliability is a first-class variable: the
highest-quality low-cost model loses 21\% of images to provider-side
aborts, one third of the total inference spend of \$389 is consumed by
failed attempts, and the released benchmark itself requires a
documented reconciliation before its numbers can be compared across
papers. These results establish the single multimodal model as the
decisive component of agentic reaction extraction and provide a
reproducible template for evaluating such pipelines. A comparison with
the sixteen-figure development phase is provided in the appendix. All
data, code, run transcripts, and per-image scores are released as
open-source software.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The construction of structured reaction databases from the published
chemical literature remains one of the key challenges on the road to
data-driven chemistry. The CAS REGISTRY passed 279 million unique
substances in early 2025,\citep{CAS2025} yet almost all published
chemistry remains locked inside PDFs whose text and figures are not
intrinsically machine readable.\citep{Rajan2021} Trusted reaction
databases are still populated by hand, which is slow, expensive, and
prone to transcription error.\citep{Hawizy2011} Therefore, only a small
fraction of the published reaction record reaches the structured form
that retrosynthesis, condition-prediction and yield-regression
models,\citep{SchillingWilhelmi2025,Schwaller2022} and reaction-class
analysis tools such as Rxn-INSIGHT,\citep{Dobbelaere2024} require.
Figure~\ref{fig:trend} quantifies the resulting gap for the fields
surrounding this work: annual publication output in flow, organic and
sustainable chemistry now exceeds 50{,}000 Web of Science papers,
whereas fewer than 2{,}000 machine-readable datasets are deposited per
year, more than an order of magnitude fewer. Vision--language models
(VLMs), general-purpose models that read text and images jointly,
embedded in tool-using agentic loops are currently the most promising
route to automating this extraction, and multi-agent systems such as
ChemEAGLE\citep{Chen2025ChemEagle} report state-of-the-art results on
their own benchmarks.

\begin{figure}[htb]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figures/fig_literature_trend}
\caption{Annual number of Web of Science papers (top) and DataCite
datasets (bottom) matching topic queries in flow chemistry, organic

\label{fig:trend}
\end{figure}

However, it remains unclear whether such reported results survive
rigorous evaluation, for two reasons. First, the multi-agent
architecture and the multimodal backbone are rarely varied
independently, so the contribution of the orchestration cannot be
separated from that of the model. Second, agentic pipelines are
typically developed and evaluated on the same benchmark, so reported
scores mix genuine capability with development-set adaptation. Both
concerns can be addressed with the standard machinery of rigorous
evaluation: a frozen system, a held-out test set, and an analysis plan
fixed in advance.

In this work, we apply that machinery to chemical reaction extraction.
A deliberately minimal single-agent pipeline (one VLM, an RDKit-backed
SMILES validator, a schema-validated submission tool, and one system
prompt) is frozen byte-for-byte and evaluated on every scoreable figure
of the public ChemEAGLE benchmark release outside its development set:
a 305-image held-out test set carrying 2{,}899 gold reactions across
four heterogeneous ground-truth subsets. Four multimodal models across
two provider generations are compared behind the identical pipeline,
and the seven-agent ChemEAGLE reference system is re-run on its own
complete benchmark under the same scorer. The evaluation is
pre-registered in the operational sense, with the pipeline, scoring
rules, and analysis plan committed publicly before the first scored
run and every subsequent change recorded as a dated amendment.
Section~\ref{sec:benchmark} provides the first complete accounting of
the public release, including every repair and exclusion;
Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the frozen pipeline, the model
roster, and the five frozen scoring rules;
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports full-benchmark scores with bootstrap
confidence intervals, the same-scorer re-run of the reference system,
and a billing-verified cost and reliability analysis; and
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the implications. The code,
benchmark builder, run transcripts and per-image scores are available
as open-source software on
\url{https://github.com/kahuysen/thesis_final_Kasper_Huysentruyt}.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Reaction extraction.} For over a decade, extraction
pipelines relied on dictionary lookups, hand-written grammars and small
machine-learning taggers trained on manually annotated corpora;
ChemicalTagger, ChemDataExtractor and OSCAR exemplify this
generation.\citep{Hawizy2011,Mavracic2021,Jessop2011} They are accurate
on narrowly scoped inputs but scale poorly across the heterogeneous
formats of contemporary chemistry papers. A second generation attacked
the figure directly with purpose-trained deep networks: so-called
optical chemical structure recognition (OCSR) models that translate a
drawn structure into a
graph,\citep{Qian2023MolScribe,Chen2024MolNexTR,Rajan2023DECIMERai}
and diagram parsers that recover the reaction topology around
them,\citep{Qian2023,Wilary2023RDE} assembled into document-level stacks
such as OpenChemIE.\citep{Fan2024OpenChemIE} These systems are accurate
within their training distribution. However, they need task-specific
supervision for every new figure convention, and their coverage falls
far short of what resources such as the Open Reaction Database are
designed to hold.\citep{Kearnes2021ORD}

\paragraph{Agentic VLM extraction.} General-purpose VLMs shifted the
landscape: a single multimodal model can read a reaction figure end to
end without task-specific fine-tuning, as MERMaid demonstrates for
reaction mining across whole PDFs.\citep{Leong2025MERMaid} Agentic
frameworks add a decision loop, structured output schemas, and external
tools (RDKit,\citep{RDKit} OCSR models,\citep{Chen2024MolNexTR} and
name-to-structure resolvers\citep{Lowe2011,Kim2016,CIR2024}) in the
spirit of tool-augmented chemistry agents such as
ChemCrow.\citep{Bran2024ChemCrow} ChemEAGLE\citep{Chen2025ChemEagle}
is the representative multi-agent instance: a planner, six specialist
agents and two observers over a chemistry toolchain, evaluated on a
benchmark of 324 figure graphics released alongside the paper. This
work uses that release in full.

\paragraph{Evaluation practice.} The machine-learning community's
standard defences against overfitting an evaluation, namely held-out
test sets untouched during development and analysis plans fixed before
the data are seen, are rarely applied to agentic-pipeline papers, which
typically report a single headline score on a benchmark the pipeline
was developed against. In this work, both defences are adopted: the
development figures are excluded from the headline set, and the
configuration, scoring rules and analysis plan were committed to a
public repository before the first scored run, with every subsequent
change recorded as a dated amendment committed before the run it
affects.

\section{The full ChemEAGLE benchmark}
\label{sec:benchmark}

\subsection{Release contents and accounting}
\label{sec:accounting}

The public ChemEAGLE release (\texttt{Benchmark.zip}, md5
\texttt{611d4c01\ldots{}422172}) contains 324 figure images and four
ground-truth files of heterogeneous provenance and schema, which to our
knowledge have not previously been jointly accounted for.
Table~\ref{tab:accounting} summarizes the release as scored in this
work. \texttt{GT1} holds 142 record-schema entries, i.e.\ reactions
with reactant, condition and product SMILES (simplified
molecular-input line-entry system) strings plus labels, of which 139
name unique images: two entries are exact duplicates and one carries no
file name at all. \texttt{GT2} covers 78 R-group-table figures with a
reaction template plus letter-keyed concrete variants; it annotates
structures only, with no conditions. \texttt{GT3} and \texttt{GT4} are
CSV tables (182 rows over 38 images; 727 rows over 66 images):
\texttt{GT3} lists reactant, product and catalyst SMILES, whereas
\texttt{GT4} adds catalyst text, yield, ee, dr, er, solvent and
temperature fields and includes wildcard template rows. The four files
are disjoint by image.

Reconciling the files against the shipped images requires a small set
of deterministic repairs, each applied by the md5-pinned builder and
logged to a machine-readable exclusions file: four ground-truth file
names are misspelled relative to the images they annotate (a colon
where the zip has an underscore, twice; \texttt{iamge} for
\texttt{image}; \texttt{CHEM\_01\_01} for \texttt{CHEM01\_01}), and five
images are PNG files carrying a \texttt{.jpg} extension, which is
harmless to lenient decoders but rejected by providers that validate
the declared media type against the payload
(Sect.~\ref{sec:results}). After repairs, 321 of the 324 images are
scoreable, carrying 2{,}899 gold reactions; three images have no
released ground truth. Hence, the source paper's own figure of 324
graphics and 2{,}983 reactions is nearly, but not exactly,
reproducible from the public release. It should be noted that this gap
matters for anyone benchmarking against the published numbers.

To characterise the chemistry the benchmark actually probes, every
gold reaction is classified with the open-source Rxn-INSIGHT
algorithm,\citep{Dobbelaere2024} and Figure~\ref{fig:benchchem} shows
the resulting profile. The benchmark is methodology-literature
chemistry rather than patent chemistry: carbon--carbon coupling
dominates with 30.0\% of the gold reactions and aromatic heterocycle
formation ranks third with 14.2\%, whereas in the USPTO patent corpus
heteroatom alkylation and arylation leads with nearly
30\%;\citep{Dobbelaere2024} here that patent workhorse class accounts
for only 9.2\%. A further 8.5\% of gold rows are wildcard R-group
templates, i.e.\ scheme headers rather than concrete reactions, which
foreshadows the enumeration failure mode analysed in
Sect.~\ref{sec:predchem}; only 3.4\% of gold reactions resist
classification. The ring-system inventory of the gold products is
headed by benzene with 64.1\% of 6{,}637 rings, followed by oxygen and
nitrogen heterocycles. The classification scripts and cached results
are released with the code.

\subsection{Held-out design and pre-registration}
\label{sec:preregistration}

Sixteen figures of the release (all within \texttt{GT1}) were used
during the development of the pipeline: all prompt engineering, tool
design and schema iteration happened against these figures, and none
against the remaining 305. Therefore, these sixteen are treated as a
development set and excluded from the headline evaluation, which
covers the \textbf{305 held-out scored images} (the 123
non-development \texttt{GT1} figures plus all 182
\texttt{GT2}--\texttt{GT4} figures). A comparison between
development-set and held-out scores is provided in
Appendix~\ref{app:dev}.

The evaluation was pre-registered in the operational sense: the
benchmark builder, the scoring rules of Sect.~\ref{sec:methods}, the
run plan (models, order, retry policy, failure handling) and the
analysis plan were committed to the public repository and pushed
\emph{before} the first scored full-benchmark run (commit
\texttt{054c6b0}). Changes forced by pre-run smoke tests or by
operational reality, namely raising the per-round completion-token cap
for reasoning models, provider pinning, and two model substitutions,
were each recorded as a dated amendment in the run plan and committed
before the run they affect. We committed in advance to publishing the
results regardless of their direction.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{The frozen single-agent pipeline}
The pipeline is deliberately minimal and was frozen byte-for-byte at
the end of the development phase: one
multimodal model, two tools (an RDKit-backed\citep{RDKit} SMILES
validator and a Pydantic-validated \texttt{submit\_extraction}
terminator), and one system prompt, used byte-for-byte identically for
every model. The agent receives the figure image and iterates freely:
the validator may be called any number of times, parser errors are
returned as tool results, and the loop terminates when a schema-valid
extraction (reactions with reactant, condition and product entries) is
submitted. The loop is implemented once against the OpenAI-compatible
chat-completions interface and reaches every model through OpenRouter,
a routing service that forwards each request to a serving provider.
The provider actually used is recorded in every per-image metadata
sidecar, and where a model is served at materially different
quantizations, the run pins the provider. It is found that median tool
use in the full runs is 8 to 12 validator calls over 3 to 4 model
rounds per figure, which confirms that all models exercise the loop
agentically rather than answering single-shot.

\subsection{Models}
No decoding or reasoning parameters are
tuned: every model runs at its provider defaults, so the comparison is
of models as deployed, not of models at their individually optimal
configuration. One harness limit was changed by amendment before any
scored run: the per-round completion-token cap was raised uniformly
from the development-phase value of 8{,}192 to 16{,}384 after smoke tests showed
that models with reasoning enabled by default exhaust the lower cap on
hidden thinking before emitting their first tool call. It should be
noted that the cap is a harness property, not a model setting, and that
holding it too low silently disables such models.

The final roster pairs each development-phase model with a
current-generation counterpart behind the same pipeline: Claude
Opus~5\footnote{Substituted for Opus~4.7 by a dated amendment;
development-phase reference numbers for the Claude family are given in
Appendix~\ref{app:dev}.} and Gemini~3.7~Flash (current generation), and
Gemini~3~Flash and GPT-5.4 (development-phase models, unchanged). A
pre-registered fifth slot for an open-weight model was abandoned by
dated amendments after three candidates proved unusable \emph{as
commercially served}, for three distinct reasons. The provider-default
thinking of Qwen3.8-27B consumes 16 to 21k tokens per round and
terminates reliably only on one bf16 endpoint at a 32k cap,
corresponding to approximately 15 to 20 hours and \$65 to \$100 per
pass. Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B, run for 160 images before discontinuation,
scored a partial-match F1 of 0.067: structurally well-formed
submissions whose molecules are simply wrong, on quantized (fp8)
endpoints. Qwen3.6-27B, an open-weight model that performed reasonably
during the development phase, now exceeds seven minutes per round as
served. Therefore, the open-weight comparison at scale is an open
problem in serving, not only in modeling.

\subsection{Scoring}
The metric definitions were fixed during the development phase: SMILES are
canonicalised through RDKit with wildcard-isotope normalisation;
set-based reactant/product intersection-over-union (IoU) is computed;
soft, hard, constitution and partial reaction matching are applied with
Hungarian assignment, with partial-match F1 (Jaccard $\geq 0.5$,
stereochemistry stripped) as the headline metric; and strict and
lenient condition matchers are run alongside. These definitions are
applied under five rules frozen in the run plan before any scored run.

(1)~Every scored image in the manifest counts: a missing, crashed or
never-submitting prediction is scored as an empty extraction, i.e.\ as
zeros. This deliberately tightens the development-phase convention,
which dropped failed images from the quality denominator and was
optimistically biased for systems that failed on hard figures; here,
failure is part of the measurement. (2)~Structural metrics are computed
with conditions stripped from both prediction and gold, so the four
ground-truth subsets, whose condition annotations range from complete
(\texttt{GT1}) through partial (\texttt{GT3}, \texttt{GT4}) to absent
(\texttt{GT2}), are scored on one footing; the with-conditions hard
match is additionally reported on \texttt{GT1}. (3)~Condition metrics
are computed only where the gold annotates at least one condition.
(4)~Wildcard rule: a predicted R-group template reaction (one
containing wildcard atoms) is dropped before scoring if and only if the
image's gold contains no wildcard reactions. Hence, a model is neither
punished for correctly reporting a template the annotation chose not to
include, nor excused from one the annotation does include.
(5)~Uncertainty on every macro-averaged headline number is a bootstrap
95\% confidence interval over images (10{,}000 resamples, fixed seed).

The scorer was validated before the first full run: applied to the
canonical development-phase reference runs it reproduces their
partial-match F1 to three decimals (Appendix~\ref{app:dev}), and it
returns exactly 1.0 for gold-as-prediction and 0.0 for empty
predictions on every ground-truth subset.

\subsection{Execution}
Models are run cheapest first, each scored before the next starts, with
bounded concurrency and in-run backoff on rate limits. After each full
pass, one re-invocation retries the remaining failures (the runner
skips completed images, so retry rounds are idempotent); anything still
failing after that is scored as zeros under rule~(1). Provider-reported
input and output token counts, wall time, tool-call counts and the
serving provider are recorded per image, and reported costs are
reconciled against the provider's billing exports rather than against
token counts alone; the cost analysis is presented in
Sect.~\ref{sec:results}.

It is found that the subset pattern is model-independent in shape: for
every capable model, the dense \texttt{GT4} condition-survey tables are
the hardest archetype (0.520 to 0.666) and the \texttt{GT3}
single-reaction figures among the easiest (0.705 to 0.798), with the
R-group tables of \texttt{GT2} in between. Two observations stand out.
First, Claude Opus~5 is strongest precisely on the R-group tables
(0.803), the figure archetype for which ChemEAGLE deploys two dedicated
substitution agents, which suggests that a strong backbone internalises
what the orchestration was built to compensate for. Second, the gap between the Gemini generations is
concentrated in extraction quality per completed image rather than in
the subset profile, which shifts little; what changed most between the
two is reliability (Sect.~\ref{sec:failures}).

\subsection{Cost--quality at scale}
Figure~\ref{fig:costquality} shows cost against held-out quality for
all five systems. The per-image economics reshuffle at the top. All
costs here are billed, all-in figures from the released provider
exports, with failed attempts included. Claude Opus~5 leads on quality
at \$0.84 per successful image, driven by output verbosity (a median of
8{,}770 output tokens per image) and by capped failure attempts. In contrast,
Gemini~3.7~Flash delivers a partial-match F1 of approximately 0.80 on
completed images at \$0.10 all-in (\$0.04 when only successful-image
tokens are counted).

Two structural cost findings generalise beyond these models. First, in
an agent loop, the billed input is dominated by conversation
re-transmission: every round re-sends the system prompt, the figure and
the full history, so cumulative input grows quadratically with round
count, and roughly 80\% of the per-image cost of Opus~5 is re-sent
context. Prompt caching, unused by the frozen provider-agnostic loop,
would cut this several-fold. Second, failure cost is invisible to
success-based accounting, and the two released billing exports
quantify it exactly. Total project spend was \$389.26, of which
roughly one third bought no output artifact. The extremes are
instructive. The discontinued Qwen3-VL run billed \$49.46 for 160
successful images against \$4.14 of successful-image tokens: 92\% of
its spend was failure loops, corresponding to 188.6 million input
tokens across 5{,}563 requests re-sending a growing conversation at
the original 128-round budget (the motivation for the 48-round
amendment). Gemini~3.7~Flash billed \$26.34 against \$10.03 (a 62\%
failure share, from retrying its 64 persistently aborting images
across 11{,}693 requests), whereas the most reliable model, GPT-5.4,
wasted only 6\%. The same export shows 214 of Claude Opus~5's 472
calls ending at the completion cap mid-round, and \$8.36 refunded by
implicit provider-side prompt caching, a preview of the several-fold
input saving that explicit caching would yield. Therefore,
success-only cost accounting understates deployed cost by a factor of
1.1 to 2.6 depending on the model's reliability; both bases are
reported.

\subsection{Comparability to ChemEAGLE's published metric}
\label{sec:chemeagle-metric}

The headline result of ChemEAGLE on this benchmark is a soft-match F1
of 0.78.\citep{Chen2025ChemEagle} The scorer of this work implements
soft match exactly as their paper defines it, i.e.\ the predicted
reactant and product sets must coincide with gold after RDKit
canonicalisation. However, under that implementation no evaluated
system approaches their number: the best model here reaches 0.268
held-out (Table~\ref{tab:main}), and, decisively, the full-benchmark
rerun of ChemEAGLE itself (same engine family, all 321 scoreable
images, crashes as zeros) scores 0.121 soft-match and 0.352
partial-match held-out. Three considerations frame the gap. First,
exact-set matching is structurally unusable on the CSV-derived subsets
whose gold lists only principal reactants (Sect.~\ref{sec:results});
restricting soft match to the fully annotated
\texttt{GT1}/\texttt{GT2} slices raises the best model to 0.29/0.40,
still far from 0.78. Second, the release contains no evaluation code,
so the operational definition behind the published number (its
treatment of failed images, its averaging basis, whether it is
conditioned on detected reactions) cannot be reproduced or checked.
Third, the consistent behaviour of five independent systems under one
published-definition implementation, including the reference system
itself, suggests that the discrepancy lies in scoring methodology
rather than in any one pipeline. Therefore, soft match is reported for
transparency, partial-match F1, which degrades smoothly instead of
saturating, is used as the headline metric, and published-number
comparisons on this benchmark should be considered unreliable until
reference evaluation code is released.

\subsection{Ground-truth quality of the public release}
Because every system is scored against the release ground truth
verbatim, its quality bounds what any model can attain, and the defects
found are documented here. Beyond the file-level defects already
described in Sect.~\ref{sec:accounting} (three images without ground
truth, five PNG-as-\texttt{.jpg} images, four misspelled file names,
two duplicate and one nameless \texttt{GT1} entries), three classes of
annotation issue affect scores directly. First, degenerate gold SMILES
occur. As an example, the template row of the \texttt{ACScat\_2020}
optimisation table encodes its amino-acid reactant as
\texttt{N[C@@]C(=O)O}, a stereocentre whose R-group substituent has
been dropped, which zeroes the partial match of every evaluated model
on that figure identically; comparisons remain fair, but the image
contributes only noise. Second, the gold of the CSV-derived subsets is
structurally incomplete: \texttt{GT3} and \texttt{GT4} list only the
named principal reactants, so exact-set (soft) matching saturates near
zero for all models (0.006 to 0.31 in these runs) while partial
matching remains informative. Hence, exact-set metrics should not be
used on these subsets. Third, the five media-type mismatches are a
live reproducibility trap: providers that validate the declared MIME
type against the payload (Anthropic, in these runs) reject those five
images outright, so a harness that trusts file extensions silently
scores a strict provider five images of zeros that a lenient provider
is spared.

During the development phase, the ground truth of the sixteen
development figures was additionally hand-corrected; the effect of
those corrections on scores is quantified in Appendix~\ref{app:dev}.

\subsection{Failure modes and serving reliability}
\label{sec:failures}
With failures scored as zeros, reliability becomes a measured quantity,
and it separates cleanly from extraction quality. GPT-5.4 completed all
324 images (its failures were only transient rate limits); Gemini~3
Flash lost 6 images to persistent non-submission; and Claude Opus~5
lost 23, all on the densest figures, where the model keeps validating
structures without concluding within the round budget. Notably,
Gemini~3.7~Flash, the highest-quality low-cost model per completed
image, lost 64 images (21\% of the pool), almost all to server-side
aborts (HTTP~504) from the provider rather than to model behaviour,
across repeated retries over several hours. The multi-agent baseline
fails differently again: ChemEAGLE crashed on 40 of 321 images (12\%)
with deterministic exceptions inside its own vendored perception stack
(31 IndexError, 8 AttributeError, 1 TypeError, concentrated in the
MolNexTR graph-to-SMILES conversion and downstream sorting). These are
reproducible failures that no retry recovers. Therefore,
the ranking of low-cost models inverts depending on whether one reads
quality-per-completion or end-to-end yield, and a practitioner
deploying these pipelines should treat provider reliability as a
first-class metric alongside F1. Two further serving-stack traps are
documented in Sect.~\ref{sec:results}: media-type validation
differences across providers (five mislabeled release images silently
zero a strict provider), and per-round tool-call batching differences
that change loop economics by an order of magnitude.

\subsection{The chemical signature of predictions}
\label{sec:predchem}

Scores state how often systems err; classifying the predictions states
what the errors look like chemically. Every predicted reaction of all
five systems is passed through the same Rxn-INSIGHT classifier as the
gold,\citep{Dobbelaere2024} and Figure~\ref{fig:predchem} compares the
resulting class profiles. The three strongest models reproduce the
chemistry of the benchmark almost exactly: their class shares sit on
or next to the gold values throughout, so their residual errors are
made within the correct reaction class (a wrong substituent or
stereocentre) rather than by misreading what kind of chemistry a
figure shows. The two weakest systems fail in a characteristic and
diagnosable way, which is termed template collapse here: instead of
enumerating the concrete substituted reactions encoded in R-group and
optimisation tables, GPT-5.4 leaves 25.0\% of its predictions as
unresolved wildcard templates and ChemEAGLE 54.6\%, against 8.5\%
template rows in the gold, and every major class is correspondingly
starved (ChemEAGLE extracts 10.0\% carbon--carbon couplings against
30.0\% in the gold). Therefore, the reference system does not extract
wrong chemistry so much as it stops at the scheme header and omits the
enumeration its own benchmark demands, which explains mechanistically
why its partial-match score remains low despite structurally
well-formed output.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{The model, not the orchestration, decides extraction
quality.} Behind a byte-identical pipeline, the spread between the
strongest and weakest model is 0.51 partial-match F1 (0.740 against
0.228), with margins resolved far beyond the bootstrap intervals.
Furthermore, the seven-agent reference system, re-run on its own
complete benchmark under the same scorer, lands at 0.352: above the
weak-backbone single agent (0.228), but at less than half the score of
a low-cost single agent with the same cost per image (Gemini~3~Flash:
0.615 at \$0.05 against ChemEAGLE's \$0.06) and less than half of the
frontier single agent. Hence, orchestration compensates for a weak
model, but it cannot compete with a strong one. In addition, the
paired comparison of Appendix~\ref{app:dev} shows that scores on the
sixteen development figures were optimistic by 0.08 to 0.14 for every
capable model, despite those figures having been selected for
difficulty. Small hand-picked benchmark subsets remain useful for
rapid controlled ablation. However, they should not be used for
absolute claims, even difficult ones.

\paragraph{Same pipeline, newer models.} Freezing the pipeline turns
provider progress into a measurable trajectory. Within one year, the
low-cost tier moved from 0.615 (Gemini~3~Flash) to approximately 0.80
per completed image (Gemini~3.7~Flash), a quality level once reserved
for the frontier tier, whereas the quality gains of the frontier tier
came bundled with 2.4 times the output verbosity and hence 2.4 times
the cost. Progress on this task is real, but it is arriving as much
through the low-cost tier as through the frontier.

\paragraph{Reliability is part of the result.} The zero-scored-failure
rule surfaces a dimension that per-completion metrics hide: end-to-end
yield. The best low-cost model loses a fifth of all images to provider
aborts, the frontier model loses 7\% to non-convergence on dense
figures, and the weakest model is the most reliable. For deployment,
and for fair benchmarking of agentic systems generally, completion
rate, serving-stack behaviour (quantization, media-type strictness,
tool-call batching, reasoning defaults) and failure economics belong
in the reported results, not in footnotes. One third of the API spend
of this study went to failed attempts, and it is believed that the
same is true, unreported, of much of the agentic-benchmark
literature.

\paragraph{The open-weight gap is now a serving gap.} None of the three
open-weight candidates could be evaluated at scale as commercially
served, each failing differently (unbounded default reasoning,
generation-stale vision at fp8 quantization, and latency). Development-phase
results suggest that open weights can approach closed low-cost-tier
quality. However, this attempt shows that the serving ecosystem around
them, as of August 2026, does not yet support sustained agentic
workloads at benchmark scale. Self-hosted evaluation is the obvious next step and
would also remove the quantization confound.

\section{Limitations}
Five limitations bound these conclusions. First, the orchestration
axis is examined at scale through one multi-agent system (ChemEAGLE
with its shipped engine); other architecture--backbone pairings were
not re-run at 321 images because of their five-to-eight-fold cost
multiple, so the full-scale orchestration comparison covers the
reference system only. More broadly, the roster of four single-agent
models reflects a fixed evaluation budget (the total billed spend of
the study was \$389), not a claim of completeness over the current
model landscape. Second,
the release ground truth is taken verbatim apart from the logged
mechanical repairs; its documented defects bound attainable scores, so
the numbers support cross-model comparison, not absolute-performance
claims. Third, provider-default reasoning and decoding settings are
inherited deliberately from the development phase, and the model axis
therefore continues to confound model capability with vendor defaults;
the serving stack adds a further layer (provider routing,
quantization) that is recorded but not controlled beyond the
documented pins. Fourth, each model was run once per image (plus
mechanical retries): the bootstrap intervals quantify sampling over
images, not decoding stochasticity, and single-run scores for adjacent
models should be compared through those intervals only. Fifth,
condition extraction is unscored on \texttt{GT2} (no gold conditions
exist) and only partially scoreable on \texttt{GT3} and \texttt{GT4},
so the condition axis of extraction quality rests on \texttt{GT1}
alone.

\section{Conclusions}
In this work, we have evaluated a frozen single-agent vision--language
pipeline on the 305 held-out scoreable images of the public ChemEAGLE
benchmark, in a pre-registered design with the scoring rules and
analysis plan committed before the first run. The central finding is
that the multimodal model decides extraction quality. Claude Opus~5
reaches a partial-match F1 of 0.740 (0.697--0.783), the Gemini Flash
tier 0.615 to 0.629, GPT-5.4 0.228, and the seven-agent ChemEAGLE
reference system, re-run on its own complete benchmark under the same
scorer, 0.352, with the decisive margins resolved beyond their
confidence intervals. However, these conclusions are bounded by the
limitations above: one multi-agent system at scale, ground truth with
documented defects, single runs per image, and provider defaults
throughout.

Based on these results, three recommendations are made for benchmark
evaluations of agentic pipelines. First, the evaluation should be
pre-registered, with a held-out test set that is untouched during
development and an analysis plan committed before the first scored
run. Second, failures should be counted as failures: end-to-end yield,
serving-stack behaviour and failure economics belong in the reported
results, since one third of the total inference spend of \$389 in this
study bought no output artifact and would be invisible to
success-based accounting. The last recommendation is that benchmark
releases should ship their evaluation code, because without it,
published numbers cannot be compared across papers, as the gap between
ChemEAGLE's published 0.78 and the 0.121 measured here for the same
system demonstrates.

At \$0.05 to \$0.84 per figure all-in, and with low-cost-tier quality
now approaching the 2025 frontier, single-agent extraction is
economically ready for interactive chemist workflows and for the
literature-conditioning step of autonomous-laboratory platforms;
closing the reliability gap, not the quality gap, is the remaining
engineering problem.
