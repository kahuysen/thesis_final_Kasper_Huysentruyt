\begin{abstract}
Prior work established, on a deliberately hard sixteen-figure subset of the
ChemEAGLE benchmark, that the quality of agentic chemical-reaction
extraction from figures is governed by the vision--language model rather
than by the orchestration around it, with the simplest single-agent design
(one model, two chemistry tools) surpassing a seven-agent system.
Sixteen figures cannot, however, exclude subset effects, and the prompts
and tools of that study were developed against those very figures. Here we
re-evaluate the frozen single-agent pipeline on the \emph{entire} public
ChemEAGLE benchmark release: 324 figure images of which 321 carry released
reaction ground truth (2{,}899 reactions across four ground-truth subsets),
with the sixteen development figures excluded from the headline set,
yielding a 305-image held-out test set on which no prompt, tool, or
configuration was ever iterated. The configuration, scoring rules, and
analysis plan were committed publicly before the first scored run.
Behind the identical frozen pipeline we compare
\PENDING{final model list: Claude Opus 5, Gemini 3 Flash, GPT-5.4,
Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B, Gemini 3.7 Flash}.
On the held-out set, Gemini~3~Flash reaches a partial-match F1 of 0.615
(95\% CI 0.569--0.661) and GPT-5.4 reaches 0.228 (0.190--0.268), against
0.790 and 0.273 respectively on the development subset under the thesis
ground truth; Claude Opus~5 reaches
\PENDING{Opus 5 held-out partial F1 + CI; interim 0.822 over its first 118
images}. The cross-model ordering of the sixteen-figure study is
\PENDING{confirmed / partially confirmed} at nineteen-fold scale, while
absolute scores drop by roughly 0.05--0.17, quantifying the optimism of
small hand-picked benchmark subsets. We further document the public
release's data quality: three images carry no ground truth, five are
mislabeled PNG/JPEG, four ground-truth records point at misspelled
filenames, and exact-set metrics are unusable on the two subsets whose
gold annotations are structurally incomplete. All data, code, run
transcripts, and per-image scores are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The chemical literature is expanding faster than it can be curated: the
CAS REGISTRY passed 279 million unique substances in early
2025,\citep{CAS2025} yet almost all published chemistry remains locked
inside PDFs whose text and figures are not intrinsically machine
readable.\citep{Rajan2021} Trusted reaction databases are still populated
by hand, which is slow, expensive, and prone to transcription
error,\citep{Hawizy2011} so only a small fraction of the published
reaction record reaches the structured form that retrosynthesis,
condition-prediction and yield-regression
models,\citep{SchillingWilhelmi2025,Schwaller2022} and reaction-class
analysis tools such as Rxn-INSIGHT,\citep{Dobbelaere2024} require.
Vision--language models (VLMs) embedded in tool-using agentic loops are
the most promising route to automating this extraction, and multi-agent
systems such as ChemEAGLE\citep{Chen2025ChemEagle} report
state-of-the-art results on their own benchmarks.

In prior work we mapped the design space of such pipelines with a
controlled ablation on sixteen reaction figures: four architectures,
from a seven-agent system down to a single VLM with two chemistry tools,
crossed against nine multimodal backbones behind an identical prompt,
tool set and output schema. The result was asymmetric. Holding the
architecture fixed, swapping the model moved partial-match F1 by
0.41--0.58; holding the model fixed, swapping the architecture moved it
by at most 0.10, and orchestration paid off only when the backbone was
weak. The simplest single-agent design surpassed every multi-step
system, with Claude Opus~4.7 reaching a partial-match F1 of 0.85 and
Gemini~3~Flash 0.79 at a tenth of the cost --- \emph{the model, not the
framework, decides the outcome.}

Three threats to validity bound that conclusion, and all three are
consequences of the sixteen-figure subset. First, \emph{size}: with
sixteen images a single figure moves the macro-averaged score by roughly
0.05, so adjacent systems cannot be distinguished and no confidence
intervals were reported. Second, \emph{selection}: the figures were
deliberately sampled to be difficult rather than representative, so
absolute scores estimate nothing beyond the subset itself. Third, and
most fundamentally, \emph{development contamination}: the pipeline's
prompt and tools were iterated while scoring those very figures, so the
subset functioned as a development set, not a test set. This paper
removes all three threats at once by freezing the winning pipeline ---
prompt, tools, schema, and run configuration, byte-for-byte --- and
evaluating it on every scoreable figure in the public ChemEAGLE
benchmark release that the prior study never touched.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Reaction extraction.} Early extraction pipelines relied on
dictionary lookups, hand-written grammars and small machine-learning
taggers trained on manually annotated corpora --- ChemicalTagger,
ChemDataExtractor and OSCAR exemplify this
generation.\citep{Hawizy2011,Mavracic2021,Jessop2011} They are accurate
on narrowly scoped inputs but scale poorly across the heterogeneous
formats of contemporary chemistry papers. A second generation attacked
the figure directly with purpose-trained deep networks: optical chemical
structure recognition (OCSR) models that translate a drawn structure
into a graph,\citep{Qian2023MolScribe,Chen2024MolNexTR,Rajan2023DECIMERai}
and diagram parsers that recover the reaction topology around
them,\citep{Qian2023,Wilary2023RDE} assembled into document-level stacks
such as OpenChemIE.\citep{Fan2024OpenChemIE} These systems are accurate
within their training distribution but need task-specific supervision
for every new figure convention, and their coverage falls far short of
what resources such as the Open Reaction Database are designed to
hold.\citep{Kearnes2021ORD}

\paragraph{Agentic VLM extraction.} General-purpose VLMs shifted the
landscape: a single multimodal model can read a reaction figure end to
end without task-specific fine-tuning, as MERMaid demonstrates for
reaction mining across whole PDFs.\citep{Leong2025MERMaid} Agentic
frameworks add a decision loop, structured output schemas, and external
tools --- RDKit,\citep{RDKit} OCSR models,\citep{Chen2024MolNexTR}
name-to-structure resolvers\citep{Lowe2011,Kim2016,CIR2024} --- in the
spirit of tool-augmented chemistry agents such as
ChemCrow.\citep{Bran2024ChemCrow} ChemEAGLE\citep{Chen2025ChemEagle}
is the representative multi-agent instance: a planner, six specialist
agents and two observers over a chemistry toolchain, evaluated on a
benchmark of 324 figure graphics released alongside the paper. Our
prior study used a sixteen-figure subset of that release; this paper
uses all of it.

\paragraph{Evaluation practice.} The machine-learning community's
standard defences against overfitting an evaluation --- held-out test
sets untouched during development, and analysis plans fixed before the
data are seen --- are rarely applied to agentic-pipeline papers, which
typically report a single headline score on a benchmark the pipeline
was developed against. We adopt both defences here: the development
figures are excluded from the headline set, and the configuration,
scoring rules and analysis plan were committed to a public repository
before the first scored run, with every subsequent change recorded as a
dated amendment committed before the run it affects.

\section{The full ChemEAGLE benchmark}
\label{sec:benchmark}

\subsection{Release contents and accounting}
\label{sec:accounting}

The public ChemEAGLE release (\texttt{Benchmark.zip}, md5
\texttt{611d4c01\ldots{}422172}) contains 324 figure images and four
ground-truth files of heterogeneous provenance and schema, which to our
knowledge have not previously been jointly accounted for. \texttt{GT1}
holds 142 record-schema entries --- reactions with reactant, condition
and product SMILES plus labels --- of which 139 name unique images: two
entries are exact duplicates and one carries no file name at all.
\texttt{GT2} covers 78 R-group-table figures with a reaction template
plus letter-keyed concrete variants; it annotates structures only, with
no conditions. \texttt{GT3} and \texttt{GT4} are CSV tables (182 rows
over 38 images; 727 rows over 66 images): \texttt{GT3} lists reactant,
product and catalyst SMILES; \texttt{GT4} adds catalyst text, yield,
ee, dr, er, solvent and temperature fields and includes wildcard
template rows. The four files are disjoint by image.

Reconciling the files against the shipped images requires a small set
of deterministic repairs, each applied by our md5-pinned builder and
logged to a machine-readable exclusions file: four ground-truth file
names are misspelled relative to the images they annotate (a colon
where the zip has an underscore, twice; \texttt{iamge} for
\texttt{image}; \texttt{CHEM\_01\_01} for \texttt{CHEM01\_01}), and five
images are PNG files carrying a \texttt{.jpg} extension --- harmless to
lenient decoders but rejected by providers that validate the declared
media type against the payload (Sect.~\ref{sec:results}). After
repairs, 321 of the 324 images are scoreable, carrying 2{,}899 gold
reactions (Table~\ref{tab:accounting}); three images have no released
ground truth. The source paper's own figure of 324 graphics and 2{,}983
reactions is therefore nearly, but not exactly, reproducible from the
public release --- a gap worth recording for anyone benchmarking
against the published numbers.

\subsection{Held-out design and pre-registration}
\label{sec:preregistration}

The sixteen figures of the prior study are byte-identical members of the
release (all within \texttt{GT1}), so they are treated as what they
were: a development set. All prompt engineering, tool design and schema
iteration in the prior work happened against these figures; none
happened against the remaining 305. The headline evaluation set of this
paper is therefore the \textbf{305 held-out scored images} (the 123 non-development \texttt{GT1}
figures plus all 182 \texttt{GT2}--\texttt{GT4} figures), with the development sixteen reported separately
as an anchor to the prior study's numbers.

The evaluation was pre-registered in the operational sense: the
benchmark builder, the scoring rules of
Sect.~\ref{sec:methods}, the run plan (models, order, retry policy,
failure handling) and the analysis plan were committed to the public
repository and pushed \emph{before} the first scored full-benchmark run
(commit \texttt{054c6b0}). Changes forced by pre-run smoke tests or by
operational reality --- raising the per-round completion-token cap for
reasoning models, provider pinning, and two model substitutions --- were
each recorded as a dated amendment in the run plan and committed before
the run they affect. We committed in advance to publishing the results
regardless of whether the prior study's ordering survived.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{The frozen single-agent pipeline}
The pipeline is the winning design of the prior study, unchanged: one
multimodal model, two tools --- an RDKit-backed\citep{RDKit} SMILES
validator and a Pydantic-validated \texttt{submit\_extraction}
terminator --- and one system prompt, used byte-for-byte identically for
every model. The agent receives the figure image and iterates freely:
it may call the validator any number of times, receives parser errors
as tool results, and terminates by submitting a schema-valid extraction
(reactions with reactant, condition and product entries). The loop is
implemented once against the OpenAI-compatible chat-completions surface
and reaches every model through OpenRouter, which routes each request
to a serving provider; the provider actually used is recorded in every
per-image metadata sidecar, and where a model is served at materially
different quantizations the run pins the provider. Median tool use in
the full runs is 8--12 validator calls over 3--4 model rounds per
figure, confirming that all models exercise the loop agentically rather
than answering single-shot.

\subsection{Models}
Following the prior study, no decoding or reasoning parameters were
tuned: every model runs at its provider defaults, so the comparison is
of models as deployed, not of models at their individually optimal
configuration. One harness limit was changed by amendment before any
scored run: the per-round completion-token cap was raised uniformly from
the prior study's 8{,}192 to 16{,}384 after smoke tests showed that
models with reasoning enabled by default exhaust the lower cap on hidden
thinking before emitting their first tool call --- the cap is a harness
property, not a model setting, and holding it too low silently disables
such models.

The roster pairs each thesis-linked model with a current-generation
counterpart behind the same pipeline: Claude
Opus~5\footnote{Substituted for the thesis winner Opus~4.7 by a dated
amendment; the thesis-continuity anchor for the Claude family is
Opus~4.7's canonical development-set run, which scores 0.854 under this
paper's scorer.} (frontier tier), Gemini~3~Flash and GPT-5.4 (the
thesis models, unchanged), the open-weight
Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B-Instruct\footnote{Substituted for Qwen3.8-27B, whose
provider-default thinking consumes 16--21k tokens per round and only
terminates reliably on one bf16 provider at a 32k cap, making a full
pass economically impractical ($\sim$15--20\,h); the substitution is
documented in the run plan with the smoke-test evidence.}, and
\PENDING{Gemini 3.7 Flash, budget permitting} (current cheap tier).

\subsection{Scoring}
The metric definitions are those of the prior study --- SMILES
canonicalised through RDKit with wildcard-isotope normalisation;
set-based reactant/product IoU; soft, hard, constitution and partial
reaction matching with Hungarian assignment, partial-match F1 (Jaccard
$\geq 0.5$, stereochemistry stripped) as the headline metric; strict and
lenient condition matchers --- applied under five rules frozen in the
run plan before any scored run.

(1)~Every scored image in the manifest counts: a missing, crashed or
never-submitting prediction is scored as an empty extraction, i.e.\ as
zeros. This deliberately tightens the prior study's convention, which
dropped failed images from the quality denominator and was optimistically
biased for systems that failed on hard figures; here failure is part of
the measurement. (2)~Structural metrics are computed with conditions
stripped from both prediction and gold, so the four ground-truth subsets
--- whose condition annotations range from complete (\texttt{GT1})
through partial (\texttt{GT3}, \texttt{GT4}) to absent (\texttt{GT2})
--- are scored on one footing; the with-conditions hard match is
additionally reported on \texttt{GT1}. (3)~Condition metrics are
computed only where the gold annotates at least one condition.
(4)~Wildcard rule: a predicted R-group template reaction (one containing
wildcard atoms) is dropped before scoring if and only if the image's
gold contains no wildcard reactions --- a model is neither punished for
correctly reporting a template the annotation chose not to include, nor
excused from one the annotation does include. (5)~Uncertainty on every
macro-averaged headline number is a bootstrap 95\% confidence interval
over images (10{,}000 resamples, fixed seed).

The scorer was validated against the prior study before the first full
run: applied to the canonical development-set runs it reproduces the
published partial-match F1 of Opus~4.7 (0.854) and GPT-5.4 (0.273) to
three decimals, and returns exactly 1.0 for gold-as-prediction and 0.0
for empty predictions on every ground-truth subset.

\subsection{Execution}
Models were run cheapest first, each scored before the next started,
with bounded concurrency and in-run backoff on rate limits. After each
full pass, one re-invocation retried the remaining failures (the runner
skips completed images, so retry rounds are idempotent); anything still
failing after that is scored as zeros under rule~(1). Provider-reported
input and output token counts, wall time, tool-call counts and the
serving provider are recorded per image, and per-image cost is computed
from those counts at the OpenRouter rates current at run time
(August~2026); see the cost analysis in
Sect.~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Ground-truth quality of the public release}
Because every system is scored against the release ground truth
verbatim, its quality bounds what any model can attain, and we document
what we found. Beyond the file-level defects already described in
Sect.~\ref{sec:accounting} (three images without ground truth, five
PNG-as-\texttt{.jpg} images, four misspelled file names, two duplicate
and one nameless \texttt{GT1} entries), three classes of annotation
issue affect scores directly. First, \emph{degenerate gold SMILES}: for
example, the template row of the \texttt{ACScat\_2020} optimisation
table encodes its amino-acid reactant as \texttt{N[C@@]C(=O)O} --- a
stereocentre whose R-group substituent has been dropped --- which
zeroes the partial match of every evaluated model on that figure
identically; comparisons remain fair, but the image contributes only
noise. Second, \emph{structurally incomplete gold} on the CSV-derived
subsets: \texttt{GT3} and \texttt{GT4} list only the named principal
reactants, so exact-set (soft) matching saturates near zero for all
models (0.006--0.31 in our runs) while partial matching remains
informative; exact-set metrics should simply not be used on these
subsets. Third, the five media-type mismatches are a live
reproducibility trap: providers that validate the declared MIME type
against the payload (Anthropic, in our runs) reject those five images
outright, so a harness that trusts file extensions silently scores a
strict provider five images of zeros that a lenient provider is spared.

The prior study additionally hand-corrected the ground truth of its
sixteen development figures. Scoring the canonical Opus~4.7
development run against the release annotations instead of the
corrected ones leaves its aggregate partial-match F1 unchanged at
0.854, indicating the corrections concerned condition detail and
labelling rather than structures.

\section{Limitations}
Five limitations bound these conclusions. First, only the single-agent
pipeline is evaluated at full-benchmark scale: re-running the
multi-agent systems on 321 images was excluded by cost (the prior study
measured them at roughly five to eight times the single agent's
per-image spend), so the orchestration-axis conclusion continues to
rest on the sixteen-figure crossed ablation, and only the model axis is
re-examined here at scale. Second, the release ground truth is taken
verbatim apart from the logged mechanical repairs; its documented
defects bound attainable scores, so the numbers support cross-model
comparison, not absolute-performance claims. Third, provider-default
reasoning and decoding settings are inherited deliberately from the
prior study, and the model axis therefore continues to confound model
capability with vendor defaults; the serving stack adds a further layer
(provider routing, quantization) that we record but do not control
beyond the documented pins. Fourth, each model was run once per image
(plus mechanical retries): the bootstrap intervals quantify sampling
over images, not decoding stochasticity, and single-run scores for
adjacent models should be compared through those intervals only.
Fifth, condition extraction is unscored on \texttt{GT2} (no gold
conditions exist) and only partially scoreable on \texttt{GT3} and
\texttt{GT4}, so the condition axis of extraction quality rests on
\texttt{GT1} alone.
